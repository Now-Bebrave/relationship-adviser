## Verified scope and findings

The longitudinal study followed 108 working-class, dual-earner, different-sex couples in the U.S. Northeast from late pregnancy through the first year after their first child. It measured housework, childcare, perceived fairness, and relationship conflict across five time points.

- Perceived fairness of household tasks was related to conflict for both mothers and fathers: divisions seen as unfair to either partner were associated with more conflict.
- Fairness did not significantly mediate every link between changes in housework and later conflict.
- For childcare, perceived fairness mediated the association between mothers' unmet expectations about the division of childcare and later conflict; the same relationship was not found for fathers.
- Housework and childcare produced different patterns and should be tracked separately.

## Decision use

- Do not negotiate only a single “50/50” percentage. Inventory recurring housework, childcare, planning, standards, timing, and who notices and initiates each task.
- Ask both partners about fairness and unmet expectations separately from logged task shares.
- Revisit the agreement after a life transition such as birth, leave ending, job change, illness, or relocation.

## Limits

- This is one observational sample with 108 mostly White, working-class, different-sex couples who became first-time parents around 2000–2003.
- The study does not establish a universally fair division or prove that changing task shares alone will reduce conflict.
- Some measures relied on one partner's report and single-item fairness ratings.
