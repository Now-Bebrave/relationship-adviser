## Verified scope and findings

Two dyadic studies examined financial worry and perceived relationship behavior. Study 1 included 97 couples recalling a recent disagreement; Study 2 included 99 couples reporting behavior during the previous week and was preregistered.

- In Study 1, greater personal financial worry was associated with perceiving the partner as less respectful, understanding, and supportive during the disagreement. The association remained after demographic and relationship-satisfaction controls.
- In Study 2, greater financial worry was associated with reporting more negative behavior by oneself and perceiving more negative behavior by the partner. Truth-and-bias analyses indicated that financially worried participants overestimated the partner's negative behavior relative to partner self-report, while perception still contained accurate information.
- The study separates subjective financial worry from income and income volatility. Worry can occur at different income levels and should not be treated as proof that the finances are objectively unsustainable.

## Decision use

- Before deciding that a financial disagreement proves disrespect or incompatibility, separate the objective numbers, each partner's reported behavior, and each partner's interpretation.
- Do not use “stress bias” to dismiss a complaint. Verify the behavior with a specific example and check shared records.
- Move financial decisions into a written worksheet so memory, accusation, and cash-flow facts are not argued as one issue.

## Limits

- Both studies are correlational and cannot show whether worry caused perception or behavior changes.
- Samples were predominantly White, North American or British, and mostly long-term couples; cultural and economic generalization is limited.
- Partner self-report is not an objective ground truth and can contain its own bias.
