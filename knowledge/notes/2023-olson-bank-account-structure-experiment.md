## Verified scope and findings

In a six-wave longitudinal experiment, engaged or newlywed couples who began with separate accounts were randomly assigned to merge money into a joint account, keep separate accounts, or receive no account-structure instruction. Across the first two years of marriage, the separate-account and no-intervention groups showed the usual decline in relationship quality, while the joint-account group maintained stronger relationship quality.

The abstract reports three supported mechanisms across experimental and correlational work: greater satisfaction with how the couple handles money, stronger financial-goal alignment, and stronger communal norms rather than strict scorekeeping.

## Decision use

- Treat account structure as a behavioral environment that can change transparency, coordination, and whether spending feels communal or transactional.
- Before merging, disclose income, debt, obligations, credit, account access, spending expectations, and emergency-exit arrangements.
- Choose an account structure for a stated purpose and review it with observable outcomes: missed bills, hidden spending, goal progress, conflict frequency, and each person's access to money.

## Limits

- The experimental result applies most directly to engaged or newlywed couples who initially maintained separate accounts; it does not establish the same effect for every relationship stage or financial context.
- A joint account does not correct deception, incompatible goals, addiction, coercion, poor money skills, or financial abuse.
- Full merging can increase harm where one partner controls access, creates undisclosed debt, or makes leaving unsafe.
- The abstract does not establish that a hybrid account produces the same effect as the full joint-account condition.
