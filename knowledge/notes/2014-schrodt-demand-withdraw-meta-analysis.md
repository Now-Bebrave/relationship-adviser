## Verified scope and results

The abstract reports a meta-analysis of 74 studies with 14,255 participants. When demanding and withdrawing behaviors were considered together, the pattern had a moderate association with overall outcomes (r = .360). Reported associations were similar for wife-demand/husband-withdraw (r = .380) and husband-demand/wife-withdraw (r = .392). The association was larger in distressed or clinical samples (r = .413) than in nondistressed samples (r = .345).

The abstract also reports associations with relational outcomes (r = .423), communicative outcomes (r = .418), demographic outcomes (r = .239), and well-being outcomes (r = .249).

## Safe current use

- Treat demand/withdraw as a repeating interaction loop: one person increases pressure, criticism, or pursuit while the other avoids, shuts down, delays, or exits.
- Do not assume the woman demands and the man withdraws; the abstract reports similar overall effect sizes for the two gendered directions examined.
- When the loop appears, change the conversation process before trying to settle the disputed topic: name one issue, reduce simultaneous demands, and set a concrete return time after a pause.

## Limits

- These are associations and do not show that the interaction pattern alone caused poor outcomes.
- A meta-analytic average does not diagnose either partner or decide who is substantively right in a specific conflict.
- The abstract does not provide enough detail here to make individual predictions from every moderator, coding method, culture, relationship type, or study design.
- Withdrawal used to regulate arousal and followed by a reliable return is different from indefinite avoidance or punishment; individual studies and context must separate them.
